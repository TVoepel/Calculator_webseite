Formula & Sources

pH = −log₁₀[H⁺] pOH = −log₁₀[OH⁻] pH + pOH = 14 (at 25 °C, Kw = 10⁻¹⁴) Henderson-Hasselbalch: pH = pKa + log₁₀([A⁻] ÷ [HA]) Required ratio: [A⁻]/[HA] = 10^(pH − pKa)

Buffer capacity is greatest when pH is close to the pKa value (a ratio close to 1:1) – outside ±1 pH unit from the pKa, a buffer has little effect.

Sources: IUPAC – Gold Book (pH definition), NIST, Wikipedia – pH

In Practice

In the lab, an acetate buffer (pKa 4.76) or phosphate buffer (pKa 7.20) is often used to keep a solution stable at a specific pH – for example for enzyme reactions or cell cultures.

Aquarium and pool water is regularly tested for pH; converting between pH and H⁺ concentration helps put small pH changes (which lie on a logarithmic scale) into realistic perspective.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you calculate pH from H+ concentration?

pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]. Example: [H⁺] = 10⁻⁷ mol/L gives pH 7 (neutral, as with pure water at 25 °C).

What is the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation?

pH = pKa + log₁₀([A⁻]/[HA]). It calculates the pH of a buffer solution from the pKa of the acid and the ratio of base to acid.

How do you prepare a buffer solution with a specific pH?

You choose an acid whose pKa is as close as possible to the desired pH, then use the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation to calculate the required ratio of base to acid: Ratio = 10^(pH − pKa).

What is the relationship between pH and pOH?

At 25 °C, pH + pOH = 14, since the ion product of water is Kw = 10⁻¹⁴. At other temperatures, this value deviates slightly.

What does buffer capacity mean?

Buffer capacity describes how much acid or base can be added before the pH changes noticeably. It is highest when pH ≈ pKa (a base:acid ratio close to 1:1).
