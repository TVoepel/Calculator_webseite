In Practice: When You Need to Convert Density

When buying materials (steel beams, sheet metal, plastic granulate), you can quickly calculate the expected weight from volume and density – useful for transport or load-bearing capacity planning.

In chemistry and physics, density is used to check the purity or composition of liquids (e.g. alcohol content via density, battery acid condition via acid density).

Abroad or in English-language datasheets, densities are often given in lb/ft³ or lb/in³, while European standards use kg/m³ or g/cm³.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the density of water?

Water has a density of 1000 kg/m³ (1 g/cm³) at 4°C (its point of maximum density) – this reference value historically underpinned the definition of the kilogram. At 20°C it's about 998.2 kg/m³.

How do you calculate density from mass and volume?

Density = mass ÷ volume (ρ = m/V). Example: 100 g of mass and 10 cm³ of volume give a density of 10 g/cm³.

Which materials are denser than water?

Most metals: aluminum (2.70 g/cm³), steel (approx. 7.85 g/cm³), copper (8.96 g/cm³), gold (19.3 g/cm³). Many types of wood (e.g. balsa ~0.15 g/cm³) and oils, by contrast, are lighter than water and float.

Why does density change with temperature?

When heated, the volume of a substance usually expands while its mass stays the same – so density decreases as temperature rises. Water is an exception between 0°C and 4°C (the density anomaly).

What is the difference between solid density and bulk density?

Solid density refers to the compact material without voids, while bulk density applies to loose bulk material (e.g. sand, gravel, granulate) including the air gaps between particles – bulk density is therefore always lower than the solid density of the same material.
