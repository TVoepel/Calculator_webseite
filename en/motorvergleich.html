Formula & Sources

1 hp = 0.735498875 kW (exact, 75 kgf·m/s using standard gravity 9.80665 m/s²) 1 kW = 1.359621617 hp Power-to-weight ratio = Vehicle weight [kg] ÷ Power [kW] Specific torque = Torque [Nm] ÷ Vehicle weight [t]

Deliberately no 0–100 km/h estimate formula: real-world acceleration also depends on traction, gear ratios, aerodynamics, and software limits, and can't be reliably calculated from power and weight alone. Power-to-weight ratio and specific torque are approximate indicators, not a time prediction.

Sources: BIPM – SI Brochure, ADAC – Vehicle Database, Wikipedia – Power-to-weight ratio

In Practice

A compact car with 110 hp and 1,000 kg (a kg/kW value of ~12.4) can feel noticeably more agile than a large sedan with 190 hp but 1,800 kg (a kg/kW value of ~12.9) – despite having far more hp, the power-to-weight ratio is almost the same.

When towing a trailer or carrying a full load, the effective weight – and with it the kg/kW value – rises noticeably, which explains why loaded vehicles accelerate noticeably more sluggishly.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you compare two motors fairly?

Beyond raw power (kW/hp) and torque (Nm), power-to-weight ratio (kg per kW) is more meaningful for acceleration, since it factors in vehicle weight. A lighter car with less power can be quicker than a heavier car with more power.

What's the difference between hp and kW?

1 hp (horsepower) equals exactly 0.735498875 kW, and 1 kW equals about 1.359621617 hp. Horsepower is the older unit, still widely used in everyday language, while kW is the SI unit of power.

Why do electric cars often accelerate faster despite having less power?

Electric motors deliver their maximum torque right from 0 RPM, while combustion engines only reach peak torque at higher revs. This is especially noticeable when accelerating from a standstill.

Can the 0-100 km/h time be calculated from power and weight alone?

Not reliably. Real-world acceleration time also depends on traction (all-wheel vs. rear-wheel drive), gear ratios, aerodynamics, and software limits. Power-to-weight ratio and specific torque correlate with acceleration but don't replace measured manufacturer figures.

What counts as a good power-to-weight ratio?

Everyday cars are often around 10-15 kg/kW, sporty models around 5-8 kg/kW, and supercars sometimes below 3 kg/kW. The lower the value, the more power is available per kilogram of vehicle weight.
